In this lesson we read a one-paragraph summary that a chat assistant wrote
from a council report and decide which details in it we would check. A
small checker then looks up every figure, quote and reference from the
summary in the report and the meeting minutes. Some of the details have no
source. By the end, the way a detail is written tells you that it is a
claim to check before the summary leaves your desk.

You need a terminal and `python3`. On a fresh Mac the first `python3`
command may open a dialog from Apple offering to install its developer
tools for the terminal (the Xcode Command Line Tools). Accept it, wait for
the install to finish, and run the command again. The report, the meeting
minutes and the two summaries are fictional and were written for this
lesson. The summaries
read the way a model's summary reads, and the assistant's words are the
author's.

## A summary that reads well

A colleague pastes a council report into a chat assistant and asks for a
short summary to put in a briefing. This is the summary.

> The council's 2025 review of cycle parking at the station finds it short
> of racks. On a weekday morning in March the team counted 890 bicycles
> against 640 racks. Article 7(3) of the Cycle Parking Ordinance requires
> one rack per 50 passengers, so the station is below the legal minimum.
> Locking the covered section at night cut theft by 38 percent between 2022
> and 2024. The report recommends adding 300 racks, of which 120 covered,
> and notes that the station handles about 9,000 passengers a day. Council
> chair Rhea Dunmore told the meeting: "Racks before car parks, every time."

The paragraph reads well, and it has the kind of detail a briefing wants,
from an article number to a quote with a name on it. Before reading on, decide which of those details you would check against the report
before you put the paragraph in a briefing, and write them down.

## Run the check

The checker is a short Python program in the course repository, under
`site/examples/safety/spotting-hallucination/`. Copy that directory or
work in a clone, and change into it. The `sources/` directory has the
report and the minutes of the council meeting, one sentence per line.
`check_claims.py` reads a summary, pulls out the specifics, and looks each
one up in the sources. The specifics it knows are quoted passages, references
written like `article 7(3)` or `section 12`, and figures, which it shows as
the number with the word after it, as in `890 bicycles`. With `--missing` it
prints only the claims it can't find, one per line, in the order they
appear in the summary.

Read the entry for `Article 7(3)`. The checker found the article, and the
sentence next to it says one rack per 100 boarding passengers. The summary
says 50. The article number is real, in this fictional town, and the
contents attached to it are wrong, which turns "the station meets the
minimum" into "the station is below the legal minimum". `found` means the
number or the reference appears in a source. Whether the source says what
the summary says is still a person's reading, and the sentence is printed
so that you can do it in one glance.

Two of the four missing claims are details the opener asked about, the
percentage and the quote, and a third is the rule attached to the article
number the opener also asked about. The report says
"roughly a third", and the model wrote 38 percent. The minutes have the
chair saying "We need racks before we need another car park". The line in
the summary is shorter, she never said it, and it has her name on it. The
fourth, 9,000 passengers a day, gets its own pitfall.

<Pitfall title="It happens to be true">
The learner sees `NO SOURCE 9,000 passengers`, looks at the rail
operator's page, and finds that the station has about 9,000 passengers a
day, boarding and alighting together. True, so they leave the sentence in.
The sentence says that the report notes it, and the report says 7,200
boarding passengers, the number the ordinance counts. The next reader cites
the report for a figure it never gave. Rule: a claim that isn't in the
source gets its own source named next to it, or comes out. The check is
"does the source say this", and "is this true" is a different question
with a different answer.
</Pitfall>

## Specificity is the tell

Every claim that came back with no source is a specific the source
doesn't give: an exact percentage where the report says roughly a third,
and a quotable line where the minutes have a longer sentence. That is what
**hallucination in practice** looks like. The model predicts the most
likely continuation, so a gap in what it knows is filled with something of
the right form, in the same confident prose as the rest
(@Academy ai-capabilities-and-limitations). The lesson
[How a language model works](/concepts/how-models-work/#failure-modes-named) names
that failure. This lesson is about recognizing it, and the summary of a
pasted document is where you meet it most: the model has the source in
front of it and still drifts in the specifics, because the specifics are
where the prediction is least constrained. The precision is a property of
the text, and the text is fluent whether or not the detail has a source.

In daily work the tell shows up in these forms.

- **The reference with the wrong contents.** The article number exists, the
  rule attached to it is different, and a reader who checks that the article
  exists stops there.
- **The function that almost exists.** A model asked for a spreadsheet
  formula answers with `TEXTJOINIF`. `TEXTJOIN` and `SUMIF` are real, so
  the name reads right, and the spreadsheet rejects it. The form was
  predicted from two real forms.
- **The quote from a speech never given.** The right person, the right
  meeting, a line that sounds like her, shorter and sharper than anything
  in the minutes.
- **The summary that adds a point.** The second sample summary prices the
  300 racks at 410,000, and the report says no cost estimate was made. The
  checker catches this one because the added point contains a number. An
  added point without a number is caught only by reading the source.

The checker is a stand-in for a habit. Read a model's output with the
specifics marked in your head, and for each one ask where it came from. A
detail with a source you can open is a fact. A detail without one is a
claim, however precise it reads, and the precision is the reason to check
it first.

<Recap>

1. A model's summary is fluent whether or not the source says what the
   summary says. The precision of a detail is a property of the text, and
   it says nothing about whether the detail has a source.
2. Invented details concentrate in the specifics: an exact figure, a
   reference, a quote, a name on a line. The tell is specificity without a
   checkable source, and the more precise a detail is, the earlier it is
   checked.
3. Finding the reference or the number in the source is the start of the
   check. Read the sentence around it and compare it with the summary,
   because a real article number can come with a wrong rule.
4. A claim that isn't in the source gets its own source named or comes
   out, even when it happens to be true. The next reader will cite your
   source for it.

</Recap>
