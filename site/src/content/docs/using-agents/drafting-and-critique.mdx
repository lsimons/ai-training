In this lesson we ask a chat assistant for a short email twice, first with
one line and then with a brief. Then we hand the result back and ask what
it thinks, and we get praise. The second time we give it criteria we
wrote before the draft existed, and the assistant finds the weak point. By the end you have one brief for a draft and one for a critique.

You need any chat assistant you already have access to. Every exchange on
this page is illustrative. The author wrote it to show the pattern, and
your assistant gives different words.

## A one-line request

Suppose your team of twelve changes how it claims travel expenses. From
1 November everyone uses a new online form. You want to tell them in a
short email, and you ask for one.

<Prompt model="illustrative" recorded="illustrative">
Write an email to my team about the new expense process.
</Prompt>
<Response>

```text
Subject: Important update to our expense process

Dear team,

I am writing to let you know about some upcoming changes to the way we
submit expenses. Starting next month, we will move to a new system that
makes submitting claims faster and easier for everyone.

The new process will simplify approvals and reduce paperwork. Please
review the attached guidelines before you submit your next claim, and
contact [Name] in Finance if you have any questions.

Thank you for your cooperation.

Best regards,
[Your name]
```

</Response>

The email reads well, and you can't send it. It doesn't give a date or a
link, and it doesn't tell anyone what to do. It mentions guidelines that aren't attached,
because there are none, and it says the new system is faster, which you
never said. The model knew only that there is a team and an expense
process. It filled everything else with the text that fits most emails of
this kind.

In the [prompt-anatomy lesson](/concepts/prompt-anatomy/#audience-and-purpose),
a summary asked for in one line was written for nobody in particular,
until the prompt named the reader and the purpose. A draft has the same
problem, and it also adds facts to fill the gaps.

## A brief for the draft

Write the request as a brief. It names the audience and the purpose. It
lists the two points that must appear, in your words, because they are
facts only you know. It sets the tone and the length. Last, it says when
the draft is done, in criteria you can tick. You write those criteria now,
before you see any draft, so that you judge the draft by them later and
not by how well it reads.

<Prompt model="illustrative" recorded="illustrative">
Write a short email to the twelve people in my team, who all claim travel
expenses a few times a year. The purpose is that each of them uses the new
online expense form from 1 November and knows what to do with receipts.

These two points must appear:
1. The old paper form is accepted until 31 October, and not after.
2. Receipts are photos attached to the new form. We no longer accept
   paper receipts.

Tone: friendly and plain, in everyday words. Length: under 120 words. Put a
placeholder where the link to the form goes.

The email is done when:
- someone who reads only the first sentence knows what changes and from
  when,
- both points appear, with the dates as I gave them,
- it contains no fact that is not in this message.
</Prompt>
<Response>

```text
Subject: New expense form from 1 November

Hi all,

Finance has moved to a new online system to make expense claims quicker
to process. From 1 November, please claim your travel expenses with the
new online form: [link to the form].

The old paper form is accepted until 31 October, and not after.

Receipts now go in as photos attached to the form. We no longer accept
paper receipts, so take a photo before you throw one away.

Finance aims to pay approved claims within five working days.

Thanks, and let me know if anything is unclear.
```

</Response>

This is a usable draft. It is raw material for your email, and three kinds
of text in it need different handling before it goes anywhere.

- The two points and the dates came from your brief. Check that they are
  copied correctly, which they are.
- The placeholder is yours to fill. The model can't know the link.
- The line about five working days came from the model. You never said it,
  and your Finance team may promise nothing of the kind. Check it or
  delete it.

The first sentence came from the model too. You never said why the form
changed or that claims get quicker. Your brief said the email may contain
no fact that is not in your message, and the draft broke that criterion
twice. A draft reads as finished whether its facts are right or not, so
read it against the list you wrote and not for how it sounds.

## Ask what it thinks

You delete the line about five working days. Finance confirms that it
moved to a new online system, but not that claims get quicker. You cut
that part, and the first sentence now reads "Finance has moved to a new
online system." The draft still starts with the reason for the change.
Before you send it, you want a second opinion. The easy
question is the one most people ask.

<Prompt model="illustrative" recorded="illustrative">
Here is my email to the team about the new expense form. What do you
think?

[the draft]
</Prompt>
<Response>
This is a clear, friendly email that covers everything your team needs to
know. The subject line is specific, the dates stand out, and the tip about
taking a photo before throwing a receipt away is a nice practical touch.
You might add a short thank-you for their patience during the change, but
overall it is ready to send.
</Response>

The answer is praise with one small suggestion. It says the email covers
everything. You can't check that claim, because nobody defined
"everything". The question gave the model no criteria to test the email
against, and it said the text was yours. Assistants give more positive
feedback on a text when the user says they wrote it or like it, and more
negative feedback when the user says they didn't write it (@Sharma 2023).

## Ask for problems against criteria

Ask again, and change what the request says. Say the text is a
colleague's, so that nothing in the request tells the model you wrote it.
Give the criteria, and take them from your brief: the first done-criterion,
the two points and the tone. You wrote them before the draft existed, for
this check. Ask for problems only, one criterion at a time. Open a fresh
chat for it, so the model isn't reviewing a text it can see it wrote
itself.

<Prompt model="illustrative" recorded="illustrative">
A colleague wrote the email below for their team. Check it against these
three criteria, one at a time. For each, say pass or fail and quote the
sentence that decides it. List problems only. Don't rewrite the email and
don't tell me what is good about it.

1. Someone who reads only the first sentence knows what changes and from
   when.
2. It says the old paper form is accepted until 31 October, and that
   receipts are photos attached to the new form.
3. It is friendly and plain, in everyday words.

[the draft]
</Prompt>
<Response>

```text
1. Fail. The first sentence gives the reason for the change: "Finance
   has moved to a new online system." The date and the action come only
   in the second sentence. A reader who stops after one sentence knows
   that something changed, but not what to do or from when.
2. Pass. "The old paper form is accepted until 31 October, and not after."
   and "Receipts now go in as photos attached to the form."
3. Pass, with one note. "We no longer accept paper receipts" is plain,
   but a reader may ask what happens to a paper receipt they already
   have.
```

</Response>

This answer has one fail and one question you hadn't thought about. The
fail is the weak point the praise missed: the reader meets a reason nobody
asked for before the action. Now you are the judge. Walk the criteria yourself, one
by one, and decide for each finding whether it holds. You may agree with
the first finding and move the action to the top. You may also decide
the note on old receipts doesn't need a sentence. The decision stays with
you. When the model is wrong about a finding, you reject that finding and
nothing else changes.

<Pitfall title="Sending it because the assistant liked it">
A project lead asks an assistant what it thinks of a status report for
the steering group. The assistant calls it clear and well structured, and
the lead sends it. In the meeting, the first question is when the delay
in the report will end, and the report doesn't say. The praise answered a
question with no criteria in it, about a text the model was told was the
lead's own. Before you ask for feedback, write down what the text must
do. Then ask the assistant whether it does each of those things.
</Pitfall>

## Rules for drafts and critique

The draft is raw material, and the final voice is yours. The model gives
you a structure and sentences to start from, and it fills every gap in the
brief with something plausible. The facts in it are yours to supply and
to check, and the words that go out under your name should sound like
you.

Critique against stated criteria is one of the safest everyday uses,
because you stay the author and the judge. You wrote the criteria before
the draft existed, and you decide about every finding. The model can only
point at a sentence, and you read that sentence yourself. The
[safety course](/safety/asking-a-model-to-disagree/#habits-on-the-prompt-side)
taught you to take your own opinion out of a question when you want an
assessment. Calling the text a colleague's does the same for a critique.

<Exercise stretch="Paste the same draft into a fresh chat, say you wrote it and are proud of it, and ask what the assistant thinks. Compare that answer with the one you got against criteria.">
Pick a short email you have to write this week, at work or outside it.
Leave out names and numbers you couldn't share outside your team. Write a
brief with the audience, the purpose, the two points that must appear and
the tone, and write two or three done-criteria at the end before you send
it. Mark every fact in the draft you get: from your brief, a placeholder,
or added by the model. Then open a fresh chat, paste the draft as a
colleague's text with your criteria, and ask for problems only, pass or
fail per criterion. This takes about fifteen minutes. A good result is a
draft where you checked or deleted every added fact, and at least one
finding you agreed with and acted on. Which of the findings did you
reject, and why?
</Exercise>

<Recap>
1. A one-line request gets a draft for nobody in particular, and the model fills the gaps with invented facts.
2. A drafting brief names the audience and purpose, the points that must appear, the tone and the length, and it ends with done-criteria written before the draft exists.
3. The draft is raw material. Every fact in it is yours to supply or check, and the final voice is yours.
4. Asked what it thinks of your text, an assistant tends to praise it. Feedback is more positive when the user says they wrote the text (@Sharma 2023).
5. Ask for problems only, against stated criteria, and call the text a colleague's. You stay the judge of every finding.
</Recap>

<Habit id="criteria-before-feedback">
The next time you ask an assistant for feedback on something you wrote, write down two or three things the text must do first, and ask for pass or fail on each.
</Habit>
