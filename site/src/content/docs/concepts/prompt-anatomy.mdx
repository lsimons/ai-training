In this lesson we take a one-line request that gets a vague answer and
rebuild it, one part at a time, into a prompt that gets a usable one. Each
round adds one part and you run the result in your own chat tool, so you see
what that part changes. By the end you have a four-item checklist you can
apply to any request.

You need any chat assistant you have access to. The lesson doesn't depend
on which product you use. Every response shown here is illustrative: the
author wrote it to show the pattern, and the component says so. Your model
gives a different text, and the comparison you care about is between your
own rounds.

## One line, one vague answer

Here is the material we work with in every round. It is a fictional email
from a supplier. Read it once now, so you can judge each answer later.

> Subject: Change to your delivery of 14 October
>
> Dear customer,
>
> Thank you for your order 4471. Because of a strike at the port, the
> pallets for this order arrive at our warehouse three days later than
> planned. We now expect to deliver on Friday 17 October between 8:00 and
> 12:00 instead of Tuesday 14 October. The two replacement chairs you asked
> about are on the same pallet. If Friday morning doesn't work for you,
> reply to this email with two other dates this week, and we book the first
> one that has a slot. Please confirm by Thursday whether Friday works, so
> we can release the pallet.
>
> Kind regards,
> Northline Logistics

Paste the email into your chat tool, and type one line above it.

<Prompt model="illustrative" recorded="illustrative">
Summarize this.

[the email, pasted in full]
</Prompt>
<Response>
This email from Northline Logistics informs the customer that their order
4471 has been delayed by three days due to a port strike. The delivery has
been rescheduled from Tuesday 14 October to Friday 17 October between 8:00
and 12:00, and includes the two replacement chairs. The customer is asked to
confirm by Thursday whether the new time works, or to propose two
alternative dates.
</Response>

You probably got something like this. It is correct and it is a paragraph
that restates the email in slightly fewer words. Whoever reads it still
has to work out what changed and what they have to do. That is the
default a model falls back on when the prompt leaves everything unsaid:
the prediction that fits most "summarize this" requests in its training
text. In the lesson on how models work you saw that the model has no
picture of what you want beyond the tokens you gave it, so the answer to a
vague prompt is an average of everyone's answer. Every part we add below
takes one of those guesses away from the model.

## The instruction

The **instruction** is the part of the prompt that says what to do. A
clear one names the task, the input it applies to and the constraints to
respect. "Summarize" names a task and nothing else. What counts as a good
summary here is the changes and the actions, and the model has no way to
know that unless you say it.

Rewrite the line so it says what to keep and what to leave out, then run
it on the same email.

<Prompt model="illustrative" recorded="illustrative">
Summarize the email below. Keep only what changed compared to the original
plan and what we have to do about it. Leave out greetings, apologies and
the reason for the delay. Do not add anything the email doesn't say.

[the email, pasted in full]
</Prompt>
<Response>
The delivery of order 4471 moves from Tuesday 14 October to Friday 17
October, 8:00 to 12:00. The two replacement chairs are on the same
delivery. You need to confirm by Thursday whether Friday works. If it
doesn't, reply with two other dates this week.
</Response>

The greeting and the strike are gone, and the reader gets the change and
the actions. Compare with your first round. Most of the difference comes
from the two constraints, "keep only" and "leave out", and from the one
rule against additions. A model fills any gap with a plausible default,
so most prompt failures trace back to something the instruction left
unsaid (@Academy ai-fluency-framework-foundations).

## Mark the boundary

The prompt now has two kinds of text. The instruction is yours and tells
the model what to do. The email is material for the model to work on. In
the prompt they are one stream of tokens, and the model has to guess where
one ends and the other begins. Usually the guess is right. When the
material itself contains sentences that look like requests, it isn't.

<Pitfall title="A line of the email becomes an order">
A learner pastes a longer version of the same email and types "summarize"
below it. The email ends with "Please confirm by Thursday whether Friday
works". The model answers: "Confirmed, Friday 17 October works for us."
It has written a reply to the supplier instead of a summary for the
learner, because the last request-shaped sentence in the prompt came from
the email. The instruction and the material ran together, so the model
treated a line of the material as the order. Rule: mark where your
instruction ends and the pasted material begins, and put the instruction
first.
</Pitfall>

A **delimiter** is a marker that fences off the material. Triple quotes,
a line of dashes, or a pair of tags such as `<email>` and `</email>` all
work. The choice matters less than using one at all, and saying in the
instruction which marker you used, so the model knows the fenced text is
the input and everything outside it is the order (@DLAI-1).

<Prompt model="illustrative" recorded="illustrative">
Summarize the email between the triple quotes. Keep only what changed
compared to the original plan and what we have to do about it. Leave out
greetings, apologies and the reason for the delay. Do not add anything
the email doesn't say.

"""
[the email, pasted in full]
"""
</Prompt>
<Response>
The delivery of order 4471 moves from Tuesday 14 October to Friday 17
October, 8:00 to 12:00. The replacement chairs come with it. Confirm by
Thursday whether Friday works, or reply with two other dates this week.
</Response>

On a short, polite email the answer barely changes, and that is the
point. The boundary costs you one line and protects you when the material
is long, when it contains requests, and later, when a tool rather than you
puts the material into the prompt. Delimiters are also the first defense
the safety lessons build on, where the pasted material is written by
someone who wants the model to follow it.

## Audience and purpose

The summary so far is for nobody in particular. In practice a summary is
for someone, who needs it for something, and those two facts decide what
goes in. The same email summarized for yourself, for the warehouse team
lead who plans the shifts, and for a customer who is waiting for the
chairs differ in what they keep and in how they say it. The model cannot
guess which reader you have in mind, so it picks a general one.

Add one sentence that names the reader and what they do with the text.

<Prompt model="illustrative" recorded="illustrative">
Summarize the email between the triple quotes for the warehouse team lead,
who has not seen it and needs to decide whether to move Tuesday's unloading
shift. Keep only what changed compared to the original plan and what we
have to do about it. Leave out greetings, apologies and the reason for the
delay. Do not add anything the email doesn't say.

"""
[the email, pasted in full]
"""
</Prompt>
<Response>
Northline moved the delivery of order 4471 from Tuesday 14 October to
Friday 17 October, 8:00 to 12:00, so the Tuesday unloading shift is not
needed for this order. The replacement chairs arrive on the same pallet.
We have to confirm the Friday slot by Thursday. If Friday morning is a
problem, we can reply with two other dates this week instead.
</Response>

The same facts now lead with the one the team lead acts on, the Tuesday
shift, and the confirmation is stated as something "we" have to do. The
prompt didn't ask for either of those. Naming the reader and the purpose
let the model choose them, and it chose better than it did for a general
reader (@Academy ai-fluency-framework-foundations).

## Format and length

Left to itself, the model picks a format and a length for a general
reader. A paragraph is its default, and the length follows the input. The
next step, a person scanning a chat message or a program reading the
answer, usually wants something else, and the only way it gets that is if
the prompt says so. State the format, the length and what to leave off,
in numbers where you can. "Keep it short" means a different thing to every
reader, and "three bullets, each under 15 words" means one thing.

<Prompt model="illustrative" recorded="illustrative">
Summarize the email between the triple quotes for the warehouse team lead,
who has not seen it and needs to decide whether to move Tuesday's unloading
shift. Keep only what changed compared to the original plan and what we
have to do about it. Leave out greetings, apologies and the reason for the
delay. Do not add anything the email doesn't say.

Format: three bullets, each under 15 words, no heading and no introduction
line.

"""
[the email, pasted in full]
"""
</Prompt>
<Response>
- Order 4471 delivery moves from Tuesday 14 to Friday 17 October, 8:00 to 12:00.
- Replacement chairs arrive on the same pallet.
- Confirm Friday by Thursday, or reply with two other dates this week.
</Response>

Put the first round next to this one. The email is the same and the model
is the same. The difference is four sentences of prompt, and each one
closed a gap the model would otherwise fill with a default. The next two
lessons add two more parts: one worked example of the output, for when
the format is hard to describe, and a fixed shape a program can read
(@DLAI-3).

<Exercise stretch="Take the four-part prompt and remove one part at a time, running each version. Which part changes the answer the most for your text?">

Pick a short text of your own that you don't mind pasting into a chat
assistant: a notice, a newsletter, a meeting invitation. Send it with a
one-line request first, such as "summarize this" or "what does this
mean". Then rewrite the request with all four parts: an instruction with
its constraints, the text fenced by a delimiter that the instruction
names, one sentence naming the reader and what they do with the result,
and a format with a length in numbers. Send that too, in a fresh chat, and
put the two answers side by side. Ten minutes is enough. Doing this once
on your own text is what makes the checklist stick.

A good result is one where you can point at each sentence of the second
answer and name the part of the prompt that put it there. Which part of
your first request was the model guessing at most?

</Exercise>

<Recap>

1. A one-line request gets the average answer, because the model fills
   every gap with the most common default from its training text.
2. The instruction says what to do: the task, the input it applies to and
   the constraints. Most prompt failures trace back to something it left
   unsaid.
3. Mark the boundary between the instruction and the pasted material with
   a delimiter, name that delimiter in the instruction, and put the
   instruction first. Material and instruction that run together let a
   line of the material become the order.
4. Name the audience and the purpose. They decide what the summary keeps
   and what it leads with.
5. State the format and the length in numbers. "Short" is a guess and
   "three bullets under 15 words" is a check.

</Recap>
